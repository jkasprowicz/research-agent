# Study Characteristics
- study objective: Use Lean Thinking, discrete-event simulation and design of experiments to plan an ED expansion and reduce length of stay/waiting after triage.
- healthcare setting: Canadian emergency department expansion scenario.
- patient population: ED patients represented in historical arrival and process data.
- sample size: Historical data from 1 Apr 2017 to 31 Mar 2018; number of patients not clearly reported in extracted abstract.
- study design: Discrete-event simulation case study with design of experiments.

# Data
- data source: Historical patient-arrival data and hospital operational databases.
- variables used: Beds, seats, resources by shift, patient arrivals, triage, short-stay-unit transfer and process times.
- outcomes measured: Length of stay, treated patient proportion, waiting after triage, transfer time and resource downtime.

# Methods
- statistical methods: Design of experiments with 30 simulation replicates per scenario and confidence intervals for outputs.
- machine learning methods: No machine-learning component.
- simulation methods: FlexSim Healthcare discrete-event simulation.
- queueing methods: Queue/wait outcomes modeled through DES rather than analytical queueing.
- optimization methods: Scenario optimization of beds, seats and resources under Lean principles.

# Results
- main findings: Simulation-guided Lean changes greatly improved throughput and reduced LOS/waiting measures compared with the initial planning scenario.
- quantitative outcomes: Treated patients increased from 17.2% to 95.7%; average LOS decreased from 2213.7 to 461.2 minutes; time after triage fell from 404.3 to 20.8 minutes.

# Limitations
- stated limitations: Simulation assumptions, process simplifications and context-specific scenario design.
- implicit limitations: ED expansion rather than waiting-list regulation; high effect sizes depend on simulated scenarios and local operational choices.

# Relevance for SUS Waiting Lists
- relevance score (1-5): 3
- justification: Methodologically useful for DES and operational improvement, but less directly tied to SUS waiting lists or referral management.
